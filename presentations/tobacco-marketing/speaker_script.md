# Tobacco Marketing on International Markets: speaker script

**Amir Kenenbaev · International Marketing, Corvinus · Fall 2026**  
Target length: about 10 minutes. The same text is in the PowerPoint speaker notes. [Brackets] are stage directions.

## 1. Title

(~15 s) Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today: tobacco marketing. Not the business side, the marketing itself: how you sell something that nobody lets you advertise. Let's start with a quick test.

Photo circle: right-click the circle > Format Shape > Fill > Picture fill to insert a portrait.

## 2. Raise your hand if you saw a cigarette ad this year.

(~40 s) Raise your hand if you have seen a cigarette ad this year. [Pause. Probably almost nobody.] Now keep your hand up, or raise it, if you have seen a cigarette pack this year. [Everyone.] That's the whole story of my presentation. The ads are gone. The marketing is not. It just moved. Let me show you where it came from.

## 3. The most famous ad in history

(~50 s) Meet the Marlboro Man. In the early 1950s Marlboro was a cigarette for women, sold as 'Mild as May'. In 1954 the Leo Burnett agency didn't change the cigarette at all. They changed the story: a cowboy, freedom, wide open spaces. And this story worked everywhere: you don't need to speak English to understand a cowboy. Marlboro became one of the best-selling brands in the world, with almost the same image in every country. So lesson one: people don't buy a product, they buy a meaning. And a simple, strong symbol is what makes a brand global.

Picture suggestion: a classic Marlboro Man print ad.

## 4. Then the cowboy got a cartoon friend

(~50 s) In 1988 the cowboy got a cartoon friend: Joe Camel, a cool camel with sunglasses. The company said the ads were for adult smokers. But in 1991 a study showed six-year-olds a set of logos, and they recognised Joe Camel as well as Mickey Mouse. After lawsuits and public pressure, the camel disappeared in 1997. Lesson two: if you want to know who an ad is really for, don't read the strategy document. Look at the ad. A cartoon speaks to children. Remember this camel, he comes back later.

Picture suggestion: a Joe Camel ad.

## 5. Then the world said

(~40 s) Then the world said: enough. Today most countries ban tobacco ads on TV, on billboards, in magazines, and the sponsorship of sports and festivals. So imagine you're the marketing manager. Your favourite tools are gone. Do you stop marketing? Of course not. When you ban a channel, the marketing doesn't stop. It moves to the next channel that is still open. And the one channel still in every customer's hand is: the pack.

## 7. Same cigarette, four different stories

(~60 s) Here are the answers. A is the USA: the logo, the colours, the whole front is still marketing. B is Kyrgyzstan: half brand, half picture warning. C is Hungary, the one you see in every Nemzeti Dohánybolt: no logo, no colours, just the name. And D is Australia, the first country with plain packs, in a colour smokers rated the least attractive, so people call it 'the ugliest colour in the world'. Now think about what this means for a global brand. In class we talk about standardisation versus adaptation, usually because of culture. Here the brand would love to use one global pack. It can't, because every country's law decides how much of the pack it may use. That's lesson three: for a regulated product, the legal environment, not culture, forces adaptation.

## 8. When you can’t change the ad, change the taste

(~55 s) Plan B: if you can't change the ad, change the product. Menthol cools the throat, so the very first cigarette doesn't hurt. Then came the click: a capsule inside the filter that you squeeze to change the flavour. It's almost a toy. And every region gets its own taste: in Kyrgyzstan people use nasvay, in the Middle East it's apple shisha. That's classic product adaptation. But notice the other side: a regular smoker doesn't need a cigarette that doesn't hurt or that clicks. A beginner does. So lesson four: flavour is not only cultural adaptation, it's also a targeting tool. It decides who your first-time customer is. And that's exactly why most countries now ban flavours.

Picture suggestion: a capsule ('click') cigarette pack.

## 9. Then came the vape

(~55 s) And now the plot twist. By the 2010s teenagers had almost stopped smoking cigarettes. The story could have ended there. Then came the vape. In 2011 almost no American high-school student vaped. By 2019, one in four did. Why so fast? Because all the rules we just saw, the ad bans, the plain packs, the flavour bans, were written for cigarettes. A vape was a new category, so for a few years the old rules simply didn't apply. When regulators caught up and cracked down on sweet flavours, the curve went down again. Lesson five: a new category escapes the old rules, and that gap is where marketing grows fastest.

## 10. Why vapes speak to kids: the 4Ps all point one way

(~55 s) So how did vapes reach kids so fast? Look at the marketing mix. Product: bubblegum, mango, cotton candy. Not flavours for a 50-year-old smoker. Package: colourful, looks like a USB stick, easy to hide from parents and teachers. Promotion: not TV, which is banned, but influencers and TikTok, where the old rules were never written. Price and place: cheap disposables at every kiosk, affordable with pocket money. The companies say vapes are for adults who want to quit. But lesson six: when every P points at the same customer, that customer is your real target. The head of the WHO called it 'a candy-flavoured trap'. And JUUL, the biggest US vape brand, paid hundreds of millions of dollars over marketing to young people.

## 11. Same playbook, new channel

(~35 s) Remember Joe Camel? Here he is on the left: a cartoon in a magazine. On the right: a sweet-flavoured vape on TikTok. Different decade, different product, different channel. But it's the same playbook: make it fun, make it sweet, and put it where young people look. Lesson two from the beginning still holds: the creative shows you the real target.

Picture suggestions: left = Joe Camel ad; right = screenshot of a vape post on social media (or colourful disposable vapes).

## 12. What this means for an international marketer

(~60 s) So what do these lessons mean for us as international marketers? Three conclusions. First: you cannot standardise the marketing of a regulated product. The Marlboro Man could be the same in every country; the pack cannot. The law decides, country by country, how much of the marketing mix you are allowed to use. Second: when you close one P, the other three take over. Promotion was banned, and the pack, the flavour, the price and the shop started doing the selling. Third, and for me the most important: targeting shows in the marketing mix, not in the mission statement. A company can say 'we only market to adults', but cartoons, candy flavours and TikTok tell you who the real customer is.

## 13. The tools are neutral. The target is not.

(~45 s) So is marketing the villain here? I don't think so. The same tools work the other way too. In the US, the 'truth' campaign used exactly the tobacco playbook: edgy ads, youth culture, rebellion. But it told teenagers that the tobacco industry was manipulating them. Studies credit it with part of the drop in teen smoking. So my view: the tools are neutral. Storytelling, packaging, flavour, influencers: they're just tools. Marketing isn't the problem. Who you aim it at is.

Picture suggestion: a 'truth' campaign ad (truthinitiative.org).

## 14. Tobacco marketing didn’t die. It moved.

(~35 s) To sum up: tobacco marketing didn't die. It moved: from the ad, to the pack, to the flavour, and now to the phone. Every time one door closed, marketers found the next one, and the next door will probably be a product we haven't seen yet. So I'll leave you with a question I find hard to answer: where is the line between marketing a legal product and marketing to children? Thank you!

# Tobacco Marketing on International Markets: speaker script

**Amir Kenenbaev · International Marketing, Corvinus · Fall 2026**  
Target length: about 10 minutes. The same text is in the PowerPoint speaker notes. [Brackets] are stage directions.

## 1. Title

(~15 s) Hi everyone, I'm Amir. Today: tobacco marketing. Not the business side, the marketing itself: how you sell something that nobody lets you advertise. Let's start with a quick test.

Photo circle: right-click the circle > Format Shape > Fill > Picture fill to insert a portrait.

## 2. Raise your hand if you saw a cigarette ad this year.

(~40 s) Raise your hand if you have seen a cigarette ad this year. [Pause. Probably almost nobody.] Now keep your hand up, or raise it, if you have seen a cigarette pack this year. [Everyone.] That's the whole story of my presentation. The ads are gone. The marketing is not. It just moved. Let me show you where it came from.

## 3. The most famous ad in history

(~50 s) Meet the Marlboro Man. In the early 1950s Marlboro was a cigarette for women, sold with the slogan 'Mild as May'. Then in 1954 the Leo Burnett agency did something brilliant: they didn't change the cigarette at all. They changed the story. They gave it a cowboy: freedom, wide open spaces, a real man. Within a few years Marlboro became one of the best-selling brands in the world. That's the power of marketing: same product, new meaning.

Picture suggestion: a classic Marlboro Man print ad.

## 4. Then the cowboy got a cartoon friend

(~45 s) In 1988 the cowboy got a cartoon friend: Joe Camel, a cool camel with sunglasses. Cartoons, as we know, are for... children. In 1991 a medical study showed six-year-olds a set of logos. They recognised Joe Camel as well as Mickey Mouse. Six-year-olds! After lawsuits and public pressure, the camel disappeared in 1997. Remember him, because he comes back later in a new form.

Picture suggestion: a Joe Camel ad.

## 5. Then the world said

(~30 s) Then the world said: enough. Today most countries ban tobacco ads on TV, on billboards, in magazines, and the sponsorship of sports and festivals. So imagine you're the marketing manager. Your favourite tools are gone. What do you do? You use the one thing still in every customer's hand: the pack.

## 6. The pack became the ad. Guess the country!

(~45 s) Here are four packs from four countries. Which country is each one from, and what's different? Shout it out!

C = Hungary: plain pack, brand name only, big picture warning.

## 7. Same cigarette, four different stories

(~45 s) Here are the answers. A is the USA: the logo, the colours, the whole front is still marketing. B is Kyrgyzstan: half brand, half picture warning. C is Hungary, the one you see in every Nemzeti Dohánybolt: no logo, no colours, just the name. And D is Australia, the first country with plain packs. Its colour was picked because smokers rated it the least attractive colour, so people call it 'the ugliest colour in the world'. Same cigarette, four completely different stories on the box. When the pack is your last billboard, every country decides how much of it you get.

## 8. When you can’t change the ad, change the taste

(~50 s) Plan B: if you can't change the ad, change the product. Menthol cools the throat, so the very first cigarette doesn't hurt. Then the industry invented the click: a little capsule inside the filter. You squeeze it, it clicks, and the flavour changes. It's almost a toy. And every region gets its own taste: in Kyrgyzstan people use nasvay, a chewing tobacco; in the Middle East it's apple or grape shisha. Today most countries ban flavours. Guess why? Because flavours are perfect for beginners. And beginners are... young.

Picture suggestion: a capsule ('click') cigarette pack.

## 9. Then came the vape

(~50 s) And now the plot twist. By the 2010s, teenagers had almost stopped smoking cigarettes. The story could have ended there. But then came the vape. Look at American high-school students: in 2011, almost nobody vaped. By 2019, one in four. That's not a product success, that's a marketing success. And look what happened when regulators cracked down on sweet flavours: the curve went down again. So how did vapes reach kids so fast?

## 10. Why vapes speak to kids

(~50 s) Four reasons, and every one is a marketing decision. Taste: bubblegum, mango, cotton candy. Not exactly flavours for a 50-year-old smoker. Look: colourful, looks like a USB stick, easy to hide from parents and teachers. Channel: not TV, which is banned, but influencers and TikTok, where the old rules were never written. And price: cheap disposables at every kiosk. The companies say vapes are for adults who want to quit smoking. The head of the WHO calls it 'a candy-flavoured trap'. JUUL, the biggest vape brand in the US, ended up paying hundreds of millions of dollars over marketing to young people.

## 11. Same playbook, new channel

(~40 s) Remember Joe Camel? Here he is on the left: a cartoon in a magazine, loved by six-year-olds. And on the right: a colourful vape in a TikTok video. Different decade, different product, different channel. But it's the same playbook: make it fun, make it sweet, put it where young people look.

Picture suggestions: left = Joe Camel ad; right = screenshot of a vape post on social media (or a photo of colourful disposable vapes).

## 12. So what did we learn?

(~40 s) So what did we learn? Tobacco marketing didn't die. It moved: from the ad, to the pack, to the flavour, and now to the phone. Every time one door closed, marketers found the next one. And I want to leave you with a question, because I don't think the answer is easy: where is the line between marketing a legal product and marketing to children? Thank you!
